Abstract

Content that ranks well today can quietly decay tomorrow, and most teams find out only after the traffic is already gone. This paper builds and validates a model that catches that decline early, using nothing but signals a team could see for themselves in the first half of any given month.

Working directly against six weeks of real FlyRank warehouse data from March 2026, spanning 151,981 pages across 44 pseudonymous clients, I trained a Random Forest classifier to predict which pages would see a meaningful click drop in the second half of the month. Every feature came from the first fifteen days only, so the model never saw anything from the period it was trying to predict. Validated on a client-grouped holdout split, meaning the fourteen test clients were never seen during training, the model reached a ROC AUC of 0.920. For comparison, a hand-written rule built from position and click-through rate managed only 0.439, which is worse than flipping a coin.

The most valuable finding in this whole project was not the accuracy number. While auditing the model's ten highest-confidence predictions, I discovered every single one belonged to a page with exactly one impression and one click, a mathematically perfect but practically meaningless one hundred percent click-through rate. I built a reliability guard that separates these thin-evidence flags into their own review tier before they ever reach a human reviewer. The result is a ranked, reason-coded action queue meant to support a content team's judgment, not replace it.

1. Problem Framing

What decision does this actually support?

A content or SEO team responsible for thousands of pages across dozens of clients cannot realistically read every page every month to check whether it is still performing. In practice, teams rely on gut feel, a rough manual spot check, or a simple rule of thumb, and most declining pages simply never get looked at until traffic has already fallen off a cliff. This project answers a narrower, more useful question: given only what a page did in the first half of March, is it worth a human editor's time reviewing it before the month ends?

Unit of analysis. Every row in this study is a single page, aggregated across its daily performance for a fifteen day window. One row means one page's early-month behavior, nothing more granular and nothing more coarse.

What the model outputs. For every page, the system produces a decline risk score between zero and one, a short reason code explaining which signal drove that score, and one of four plain-language action labels: refresh priority, monitor, low confidence flag, or no action.

Who acts on this, and how. A content editor or SEO strategist works down the ranked queue from the top and decides, page by page, whether to refresh the content, keep an eye on it next cycle, or leave it alone. The model narrows an impossibly large list down to a manageable one. It never makes the final call itself.

What a wrong call actually costs. Missing a page that is genuinely declining means lost visibility keeps compounding quietly until it becomes expensive to reverse. Flagging a page that was never actually at risk wastes a reviewer's limited time on something that did not need it. There is a third, subtler cost that only became obvious once I inspected the model's own output closely: flagging a page on evidence that is statistically meaningless, such as a single click inflating a CTR to one hundred percent, does not just waste one review slot. It quietly teaches the reviewer to stop trusting the queue altogether, which is far more damaging than any individual missed flag.

Why this genuinely needed a model rather than a simple rule. The first attempt at solving this was a hand-written rule combining ranking position with click-through rate, the kind of logic a strategist might write down on a whiteboard. Scored fairly on held-out data, that rule reached a ROC AUC of only 0.439, meaning it was actually worse than randomly guessing which pages would decline. An earlier signal audit in this same project had already surfaced part of the reason why: decline risk does not rise steadily as ranking position gets worse, as most people would assume. Instead it peaks in the middle of the ranking pack and is actually lowest at both extremes, the very top positions and the deepest, barely visible ones. A rule built on the naive assumption breaks immediately. A model that can learn interactions between several weaker signals does much better.

2. Data

Where this comes from, and what was deliberately left out

Source. All of the analysis in this paper comes from the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse, specifically the fact_content_daily_performance table, which is hosted on Hugging Face and queried directly with DuckDB over the hf:// protocol. Nothing was downloaded locally. This is the real, production-scale release referenced throughout this internship, not the smaller anonymized sample used in earlier weekly exercises.

Partition and scope. This study uses the month=2026-03 partition. After filtering to rows where gsc_data_available is true and impressions are greater than zero, that leaves 151,981 distinct pages spread across 44 pseudonymous clients.

How the feature and label windows are separated. Every feature used by the model is computed only from rows where the report date falls on or before March 15th. The label, meaning whether a page actually declined, is computed only from rows on or after March 16th. There is a hard calendar boundary between the two, with zero overlap, so nothing from the second half of the month can ever leak into what the model is allowed to see.

How decline is defined. A page is labeled as declining if its clicks in the second half of March fall below ninety percent of its first-half clicks. Under this definition, 18.7 percent of pages in this dataset are labeled as declining, which sets the base rate that every other number in this paper should be compared against.

What was deliberately excluded, and why. The future click count used to build the label itself is never allowed anywhere near the feature set. I confirmed this mattered with a direct experiment: deliberately adding that future value back in as a feature pushed the model's ROC AUC from 0.921 up to an almost suspicious 0.998, which is exactly the signature of a model being handed the answer instead of learning a real pattern. That experiment lives in this project's earlier leakage-check notebook and is the reason that feature stays out permanently.

On privacy. No client names, domains, page URLs, or private search queries appear anywhere in this repository or in this paper. Every identifier referenced anywhere in this project is a pseudonymous hash generated by FlyRank, never anything that could identify a real client or page.

3. Methodology

How the baseline, the features, and the split were built

The baseline rule. Before touching any model, I built a transparent rule a strategist could apply by hand: flag a page if it holds a top three or page one position, and its click-through rate underperforms the average CTR for its position band by more than thirty percent. That expected CTR per position band was calibrated using training data only, then applied to the untouched test set, so the baseline is not accidentally cheating by peeking at test data either.

Features. The model uses four signals, all computed strictly within the first fifteen days of March: log-scaled total impressions, log-scaled total clicks, average ranking position, and click-through rate. Logging the impression and click counts was necessary because both are heavily right-skewed, with a small number of very high-traffic pages otherwise dominating the raw distribution.

Models compared. Logistic Regression served as a simple, interpretable linear baseline, and a Random Forest with two hundred trees and a maximum depth of six was trained to see whether capturing interactions between features would meaningfully outperform it. Both models used class-balanced weighting to account for the eighteen point seven percent base rate.

Validation design. The data was split using a GroupShuffleSplit grouped by client, giving thirty training clients and fourteen fully held-out test clients with zero overlap between the two groups. This choice matters more than it might first appear. A naive row-level split would let the same client's pages sit on both sides of the split, and the model could then partly learn that particular client's quirks, writing style, or reporting cadence rather than a pattern that would actually generalize to a brand new client it has never encountered before.

Leakage checks performed. Every feature was individually confirmed to be computable strictly before the label window opens. A correlation audit was also run between each feature and the target label, and the strongest single correlation observed was only 0.18, far below the roughly 0.85 threshold that would normally signal a disguised copy of the label hiding inside a feature.

An honest note on the recall number. Tuning the classification threshold using Youden's J statistic moved the cutoff from the default 0.5 up to 0.659, but recall barely changed, dropping only from 0.9999 to 0.9996. That tells me the model's predicted probabilities are heavily bunched up near 1.0 for pages it believes are declining, rather than the threshold itself being poorly chosen. Because of this, ROC AUC, which does not depend on picking any single threshold, is the more trustworthy number to compare across methods in this paper.

5. Limitations

What this work cannot honestly claim

  • This model was validated on a single month, March 2026, and has not been tested against other seasons or time periods, so seasonal effects are entirely unaccounted for.
  • Training used only thirty of the forty four available clients. How well this generalizes to a client with a very different industry or traffic profile has not been verified.
  • An average position of exactly zero in this dataset means "no ranking data available," not literally the top spot. This affects a small slice of the test set, roughly 0.7 percent.
  • The decline label used here, a drop below ninety percent of first-half clicks, is a reasonable proxy but has not been validated against any real business definition of what counts as a meaningful decline.
  • Everything in this paper is observational and decision-support in nature. Nothing here is a causal claim about why a page declines, and nothing here claims any insight into how Google or any other search engine actually ranks pages.
6. Ranked Recommendations

Turning a risk score into something a person can actually act on

The discovery that reshaped the entire playbook. When I went back and manually inspected the model's ten single highest-risk predictions, expecting to see its strongest evidence, I instead found every one of them belonged to a page with exactly one impression and one click. Mathematically that registers as a perfect one hundred percent click-through rate, but there is obviously nothing meaningful behind a sample size of one. The model was not wrong about the math. It was simply never told that some evidence is too thin to trust.

To fix this properly rather than just noting it as a caveat, I added a reliability guard directly into the scoring pipeline. Any page with fewer than thirty impressions in the fifteen day feature window is now routed into its own dedicated review tier, low confidence flag, instead of being allowed to compete for a spot in the high-priority refresh queue.

Human review stays mandatory for every refresh priority flag. Even with the reliability guard in place, a reviewer should still confirm that elevated click activity reflects a genuinely sustainable pattern rather than a short-lived spike, and check whether the page is tied to a time-limited campaign where a later drop was always expected and is not actually a problem.

What should never be automated on top of this system. Publishing content changes automatically, deprioritizing or removing pages without a human looking first, and treating the risk score as a precise, calibrated probability of real-world traffic loss rather than what it actually is, a ranking signal meant purely for prioritization.
