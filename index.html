Which pages are about to decline — before they do?

A reliability-guarded model for flagging content decline risk in real search performance data, built on FlyRank's production warehouse.

Author: Hussain Lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring Repo: GitHub →

Abstract

Content that ranks well quietly decays — and most teams only notice after the traffic is gone. Using six weeks of real FlyRank warehouse data (March 2026, 151,981 pages across 44 clients), I built a Random Forest classifier to predict which pages will see a meaningful click drop, using only signals visible in the first half of the month. Validated on a client-grouped holdout split, the model reached a ROC AUC of 0.920, against 0.439 for a hand-written baseline rule — worse than chance. While auditing the model's highest-confidence flags, I found every one of its top 10 predictions was driven by a single-click, single-impression page reporting a statistically meaningless "100% CTR." I built a reliability guard to catch exactly this pattern before it reaches a reviewer. The result is a ranked, reason-coded action queue — decision-support for a content team, not an autonomous system.

1. Problem framing

The decision this supports: which pages should a content or SEO team prioritize for review, given limited capacity to manually check thousands of pages a month.

Unit of analysis: one page, aggregated over a 15-day feature window.

Output: a decline-risk score, a reason code, and an action label — refresh_priority, monitor, low_confidence_flag, or no_action.

Cost of a wrong call: a missed decline compounds silently until it's expensive to reverse. A false flag wastes a scarce review slot. A subtler cost, discovered directly while building this — flagging a page on statistically meaningless evidence erodes trust in the whole system, which is why thin-evidence pages get their own tier rather than a forced confident call.

Why this needed a model, not just a rule: a hand-written rule combining position and CTR reached a ROC AUC of 0.439 — worse than random guessing. An earlier signal audit had already found that decline risk peaks in the middle of the ranking pack, not at the extremes — the opposite of the naive assumption a simple rule would encode.

2. Data

Source: FlyRank ML Internship warehouse, fact_content_daily_performance table, queried directly via DuckDB over Hugging Face (hf://) — no local download.

Partition: month=2026-03, 151,981 pages across 44 clients, filtered to gsc_data_available = TRUE and non-zero impressions.

Feature window: report_date <= 2026-03-15. Label window: report_date >= 2026-03-16. No overlap.

Label: a page is declining = 1 if its second-half clicks fall below 90% of its first-half clicks. Base rate: 18.7%.

Excluded: the future click count used to build the label is never used as a feature — confirmed by a deliberate leak test: including it inflates AUC from 0.921 to 0.998.

Public safety: no client names, domains, URLs, or private queries appear anywhere in this repository. Every identifier is a pseudonymous hash.

3. Methodology

Baseline rule: flags a page if it holds a top-3 or page-1 position AND its CTR underperforms its position band's training-calibrated expected CTR by more than 30%.

Features: log-scaled impressions and clicks, average position, and CTR — all computed strictly within the feature window.

Models compared: Logistic Regression and Random Forest (200 trees, max depth 6, class-balanced).

Split: GroupShuffleSplit by client — 30 training clients, 14 test clients, zero overlap. A row-level split would let the same client's pages leak across train and test.

Leakage checks: every feature confirmed computed before the label window; correlation audit showed no feature suspiciously close to a disguised label copy (max correlation: 0.18, far below the leakage threshold of ~0.85).

An honest threshold finding: tuning the classification threshold (Youden's J statistic, 0.659 vs. the default 0.5) barely moved recall — 0.9999 → 0.9996. This means the model's predicted probabilities are heavily concentrated near 1.0 for the positive class, not miscalibrated at a single threshold. ROC AUC, which doesn't depend on a threshold, is the more trustworthy comparison metric here.

5. Limitations

  • Single-month validation (March 2026 only) — not tested across seasons.
  • Trained on 30 of 44 clients — generalization to unseen client types is unverified.
  • avg_position = 0 means "no data," not literal rank zero (0.7% of the test set).
  • The label is a proxy for "declining," not a validated business definition of decay.
  • This is observational, decision-support evidence — not a causal claim, and not a claim about how any search engine ranks pages.

6. Ranked recommendations

Fig. 3 — CTR and click momentum together account for 55.4% of the model's decisions.

The finding that reshaped the whole playbook: auditing the model's own top 10 highest-risk predictions revealed every single one was a page with 1 impression and 1 click — a mathematically "perfect" 100% CTR that carries zero real signal. I built a reliability guard: pages below 30 fifteen-day impressions are routed to their own low_confidence_flag tier instead of contaminating refresh_priority.

Fig. 4 — The highest risk scores cluster heavily among low-sample pages — exactly what the reliability guard was built to catch.

Fig. 5 — The resulting four-tier queue: 25,050 no_action, 12,189 monitor, 4,064 refresh_priority, 275 low_confidence_flag.

Human review is mandatory for every refresh_priority flag. A reviewer should check whether elevated CTR or click momentum reflects a sustainable pattern versus a short-lived spike, and whether the page is tied to time-limited campaign content.

What must never be automated: auto-publishing content changes, auto-deprioritizing pages, or treating the risk score as a calibrated real-world probability rather than a ranking signal.
